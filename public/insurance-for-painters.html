Why painters need insurance

Painting looks simple, but ladders fall, sprayers drift, a drop cloth gets missed and people get hurt. A single accident can cost more than a year of profit. Insurance is how a small contractor stays in business after a bad day.

  • It protects your business and your savings when you are held responsible for an injury or for damage to a customer's property.
  • Many customers ask for proof. Homeowners, property managers, HOAs and general contractors often want to see insurance before they sign. Having proof ready lets you say yes quickly.
  • It can help you win jobs. A customer who sees proof of insurance has one less reason to hesitate.

In many places you can legally work without some types of coverage, but then you carry all of the risk yourself. If you do not have coverage yet, you are usually better off getting it.

The main types of insurance for painting contractors

CoverageWhat it is for
General liabilityInjuries to other people and damage to a customer's property caused by your work, such as paint on a car or a ladder through a window. Ask that it includes completed operations, which covers problems that appear after the job is done.
Workers' compensationInjuries to your employees. Rules are different in every state, so ask what is required for you. Even where it is optional, some customers and general contractors require it.
Commercial autoVehicles used for the business. A personal auto policy often does not cover a work truck full of ladders and paint.
Tools and equipmentSprayers, ladders and tools that are stolen or damaged. Sometimes called inland marine.
Umbrella or excess liabilityExtra limit on top of general liability. Larger commercial jobs often ask for it.
Pollution or lead coverageStandard policies often exclude lead paint and similar hazards. Important if you work on older homes.
Builder's riskUsually bought by the property owner or general contractor for a building under construction. Ask who carries it before you start.

Bonds are not insurance. A license or performance bond is a promise backed by a surety company. Ask your agent which ones your state or customer requires.

What to look for in a painting contractor's policy

Limits

The limit is the most the policy will pay. Look at the per-occurrence limit, the general aggregate (the yearly total) and the products and completed operations aggregate.

Your real work is listed

Interior, exterior, commercial, cabinets, power washing, drywall repair, epoxy floors. If the class on the policy does not match what you actually do, a claim can be denied.

Exclusions

Read them before you need them. Common exclusions include height limits (for example work above two or three stories), lead paint, mold, stucco or EIFS, residential-only or commercial-only wording, work by subcontractors, and work done before the policy started.

Occurrence or claims-made

An occurrence policy covers incidents that happen during the policy period. Claims-made policies have reporting rules and need more care.

Deductible, names and dates

Make sure you can afford the deductible on a bad day, your business name is spelled correctly, and the policy dates are current.

Endorsements

Endorsements are add-ons that change a policy. They can add coverage, such as additional insured, or take it away.

How to buy contractor insurance

  1. Write down what you do: types of work, the tallest building you paint, how much work is commercial, whether you use subcontractors, and whether you work on homes built before 1978.
  2. Estimate your numbers: yearly sales, payroll and number of employees. Insurers price from these, so be accurate and honest. A wrong answer on an application can cause a claim to be denied.
  3. Get several quotes: two or three independent agents and at least one direct carrier. Ask each for the same limits so you can compare fairly.
  4. Ask about specialists: some carriers focus on painters and contractors and know which endorsements matter.
  5. Compare more than price: look at exclusions, deductibles and how payments work.
  6. After you buy: get your certificate, save the full policy and set a renewal reminder.

Special coverage for certain jobs

Some jobs need more than your everyday policy. Ask your agent before you bid, not after you win.

  • Additional insured: a customer, property manager or general contractor asks to be listed on your policy for a job. Your agent adds it with an endorsement.
  • Waiver of subrogation and primary and non-contributory wording: common in commercial contracts. Ask whether your policy can include them.
  • Higher limits or an umbrella for large commercial work, and a per-project aggregate so one big job does not use up your yearly limit.
  • Raising a height limit for tall buildings or lifts.
  • Lead-safe work on older homes: the EPA requires certification for many repairs and paint work in homes built before 1978. Ask about lead and pollution coverage and training.
  • Wrap-up or owner-controlled programs: on some large projects the owner buys one policy for everyone. Ask the general contractor.

Questions to ask your insurance agent

  • Does my policy cover the kind of work I actually do, including exterior, commercial and spray work?
  • What is the height limit, and how do I raise it?
  • Am I covered for lead paint, mold, stucco and EIFS? If not, can I add it?
  • Does it cover my employees, helpers and subcontractors?
  • What happens if I hire a subcontractor who has no insurance?
  • Is completed operations coverage included, and for how long after the job?
  • Can you add a customer as additional insured, and how fast can you send a certificate?
  • What is my deductible, and how do claims work?
  • Is the policy occurrence or claims-made?
  • Do my tools, sprayers and truck have coverage?
  • What would make a claim get denied, and how do I avoid that?
  • How soon must I report an accident or a possible claim?
  • What will change my price at renewal, and what does an audit look like?

Certificates of insurance (COIs)

A certificate of insurance is a one-page proof that a policy exists, showing the carrier, dates and limits. It is not the policy and does not change it. The certificate holder is who the certificate is sent to. An additional insured is someone who is actually added to your coverage. They are not the same thing. Your insurance agent issues official certificates, so ask how long they take.

Hiring subcontractors

  • Ask every subcontractor for a current certificate before they start, and check the dates and coverage types.
  • If a subcontractor has no insurance, your own policy may be asked to cover their work, and your insurer may charge more at audit time.
  • Put the insurance requirement in your subcontract and keep the certificates with the job file.

If something goes wrong on a job

  1. If anyone is hurt, call 911 first.
  2. Make the area safe and stop work near the problem.
  3. Take photos and video before anything is moved or cleaned up.
  4. Write down names and phone numbers of everyone involved and any witnesses.
  5. Do not admit fault, promise to pay or sign anything. You can say you will report it to your insurance.
  6. Call your agent or carrier as soon as you can. Many policies require quick notice, and a late report can hurt a claim.
  7. Keep receipts, messages and quotes, and do not throw away damaged items.

For homeowners hiring a painter

You can protect yourself with a few simple steps before the work starts.

  • Ask for proof of insurance and check the dates and the types of coverage.
  • Call the insurance company named on the certificate to confirm the policy is active.
  • Ask whether the painter has workers' compensation for their crew, and who is responsible if a subcontractor is hurt.
  • Walk the property together and photograph anything already damaged.
  • Get everything in writing: the scope, the price and the payment schedule.
  • If something happens, tell the painter right away, take photos and keep receipts.

Frequently asked questions

Do painters need insurance?

Requirements vary by state and by customer, but most painting contractors carry general liability insurance. Many homeowners, property managers and general contractors ask for proof before they hire.

What insurance does a painting contractor need?

Most painters start with general liability, including completed operations. Depending on the business they may also need workers' compensation, commercial auto, tools and equipment coverage and an umbrella policy. Ask a licensed insurance agent what applies to your work and your state.

Does general liability cover lead paint?

Often not. Many standard policies exclude lead paint and similar hazards. If you work on homes built before 1978, ask about lead and pollution coverage and about EPA lead-safe work rules.

What is the difference between a certificate holder and an additional insured?

A certificate holder only receives a copy of the certificate. An additional insured is added to the policy for a job through an endorsement.

Can I work without insurance?

In many places you can, but you carry all of the risk and some customers will not hire without proof. If you do not have coverage yet, you are usually better off getting it.

How can a homeowner check that a painter is insured?

Ask for a certificate of insurance, check the dates and coverage types, and call the insurance company named on it to confirm the policy is active.
